# The Burton Inn

**A Five-Year Lease and a Path to Purchase — prepared for the building owner**
**Draft v3.5 — November 13, 2026 — replaces the July 2026 version**

> *This document lays out a five-year plan for the Burton Inn: how the rent works, what the owner receives, and how the two of us would arrive at a fair price for the building. The projected cost of running the inn is itemized in the attached Exhibit D, Projected Annual Operating Budget. Figures are projections from the stated assumptions, not guarantees. There is space to write under each section.*

**The baseline is an empty building.** Today the property earns nothing and costs its owner about $19,500 a year in taxes, insurance, and upkeep. The plan below turns it into a building that pays its own way, earns a return, and has a documented value — and it takes work off the owner's desk at every step.

## The Five-Year Path

| When | What happens | What it means for the owner |
|---|---|---|
| **At signing** (late 2026) | An independent appraiser sets the building's value as it stands today. The lease is signed. | A written starting value. Taxes, insurance, and upkeep move to the tenant the following month. |
| **Years 1–3** (through 2029) | The inn reopens in stages and builds its business. | Rent every quarter and a monthly revenue report: a three-year record of what the building earns. |
| **Value review** (spring 2030) | We sit down with the three-year record and set the purchase price by the method in Section 4. | A price based on the appraisal and the record, not on a negotiation under pressure. |
| **Years 4–5** (2030–2031) | The tenant arranges financing and completes the purchase: the inn alone, or the inn and the Yellow House together. | Rent continues until closing. One buyer, one closing. |

## 1 · How the Rent Is Calculated

| Component | Amount | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Tier One** — property costs | About $19,500 a year | Taxes $8,500 (the current bill), building insurance $6,500, routine upkeep $4,500. Paid by the tenant at actual cost, so the owner is never exposed to increases. No management fee and no escrow account. |
| **Tier Two** — minimum | $1,650 a month from January 1, 2028 | $19,800 a year, fixed for the term. No minimum in Lease Year 1 while the business opens in stages. |
| **Tier Two** — percentage | 10% of gross revenue | Receipts-based and verifiable; no expense accounting involved. The owner receives the greater of this or the minimum. |

**Why this structure.** The minimum is set so the business can survive a weak year, and the percentage makes the owner an automatic participant in every good one. As revenue grows, the owner's return grows with it, without a renegotiation.

**How it is paid and checked.** The minimum is paid monthly. The percentage is paid quarterly, within a month after each calendar quarter, with a year-end true-up by March 31. The tenant sends a monthly revenue report and a certified annual statement. The owner may audit point-of-sale and booking records once a year; if revenue was understated by more than 10%, the tenant pays for the audit.

**Two honest notes.** First, 14–17% is a full rent load. Single-use restaurant benchmarks put median occupancy cost near 6% of sales (National Restaurant Association, 2024) and call anything over 10% high. The owner is paid above single-use norms, which is supportable because the same building earns several ways: an inn around the clock, a bar at night, a dinner venue, a morning coffee service, and a commercial kitchen by day. Second, coverage is the real safety measure, and it is thinner than the July figures showed. With the full cost list in Exhibit D, including a 10% contingency, the business earns about 1.9 times its occupancy cost at target and about 1.1 times at the floor. At the floor the business roughly breaks even after rent, which is why the percentage matters: if revenue falls, Tier Two falls with it, down to the minimum. The revenue behind these figures is itemized in Appendix A, and the costs in Exhibit D, which also shows Year 1 and a rooms-only year.

| Item | Carried by |
|---|---|
| Property taxes | Tenant, at actual cost (Tier One) |

| Liability, liquor-liability, and business-income insurance | Tenant (owner named as additional insured) |
| Interior fit-out (about $98,000) | Tenant |
| Fire suppression and HVAC (about $50,000) | Owner — under way now, funded by the tenant's $70,000 advance |
| Roof, foundation, and structure | Owner |
| Existing code violations, including the sunroom | Owner |

**What is left on the owner's list.** Finish the fire suppression and HVAC work already in progress, resolve the sunroom violation, and sign owner consents on permit applications. After that, the owner's part is the roof, foundation, and structure, as it is today.

**The $70,000 advance.** The tenant has advanced the owner $70,000 under the Side Agreement, and it is paying for the building systems work. The owner plans to repay it by March 31, 2027, on the Side Agreement's terms. Any part not repaid by then is credited against rent as the Side Agreement provides.

**The owner's downside.** If the business underperforms, rent falls toward the minimum rather than the tenant defaulting. If the tenancy ends early, the owner keeps the fit-out attached to his building, his new systems, and a property that has been maintained and insured at the tenant's expense. The owner has no capital in the operating company. His exposure is vacancy, which is the risk the building carries today, on a materially improved building.

## 4 · Setting a Fair Price

No one has to name a number today. The price comes from a method both sides agree to now and apply together at the value review.

**Step 1 — Today's value.** At signing, an independent appraiser we both accept values the building as it stands: empty, before the tenant's fit-out, with its current condition. This is the foundation of the price.

**Step 2 — Keep it whole.** At the value review in spring 2030, today's value is adjusted for inflation, so the owner does not lose ground by waiting.

**Step 3 — Share what the building has proven.** The rent the building earned in Lease Years 2 and 3 is converted to a value at a rate agreed now. If that rent-based value is higher than the adjusted appraisal, half of the difference is added to the price, up to a cap of 10% of the adjusted appraisal.

**The tenant's own fit-out is not added to the price.** The appraisal is taken before that work, so the tenant does not pay twice for improvements he funded.

**How the cap works, in percentages:**

| If the rent-based value comes in... | The price is the adjusted appraisal... |
|---|---|
| At or below the adjusted appraisal | With nothing added. The owner still receives today's value, kept whole for inflation. |
| 12% above | Plus 6% (half of the difference) |
| 30% above | Plus 10% (half would be 15%; the cap applies) |

**Why this is fair to both sides.** The owner is guaranteed today's value, protected against inflation, and shares in the upside if the inn proves the building can earn more. He has also received 10% of every dollar of revenue along the way. The tenant can invest and work to grow the business knowing the price cannot run away from him.

## 6 · If the Inn Is Still Growing

Five years is long enough to know whether the business works. It may not be long enough for the business to finish growing, or for a lender to see it at full strength.

- **Hotels take about three years to settle.** A Cornell study of 3,699 hotels found that occupancy takes about 3.1 years on average to stabilize, and practitioners plan on 36 to 48 months. That average means many are still climbing at three years.
- **The clock starts at opening, not signing.** If the rooms reopen in spring 2027 and the kitchen and bar around mid-year, the value review looks at about two and a half years of rooms and less than that for the kitchen.
- **Restaurants and bars run on a similar timeline.** Reliable data is thinner, but most estimates put break-even at 6 to 18 months and consistent profit at up to three years.

**Proposed.** The price is set at the value review and does not change after that. If the purchase has not closed by the end of Year 5 and the tenant is in good standing, the tenant may extend the lease once, for up to two years, on the same terms, to finish the financing. The owner keeps receiving rent throughout.

## 7 · Getting Open: Zoning and Permits

The revenue in this document depends on reopening the guest rooms and then the kitchen and bar. Where that stands:

- **Zone.** The property is zoned NB. A bed-and-breakfast is allowed only as accessory to the operator's residence, with a maximum of five rooms.
- **Certificate of occupancy.** The County's 1992 certificate covers four guest rooms over a 62-seat dining room. Whether it still holds after the closure since 2022 is an open question.
- **Sunroom violation.** An open violation on the sunroom is the likely gate on new permits. If the County lets it be resolved separately, the rooms could reopen in roughly four to six weeks (our estimate, not the County's).
- **Proposed protection.** If the guest rooms cannot legally reopen by June 30, 2027, the tenant may end the lease.

- **Inn ($110,376):** 36% occupancy at $210 a night, or 525 booked nights a year across 4 rooms. The observed Vashon short-term lodging average is 52% occupancy (AirDNA, June 2026; Bainbridge Island runs 56%), so this case sits 16 points below the market as a margin of safety. $210 is at the entry point for Vashon lodging ($221–234 cheapest-room rates on Tripadvisor; whole-home rentals average $327).
- **Cocktail bar ($79,800):** 12.5 covers a night at $38, 4 nights a week, 42 weeks. Two craft cocktails at Seattle-area prices ($16–24 each) plus a snack puts a realistic check at $38–50, so $38 is the low end. That is roughly a quarter of a 50-seat room on an average night.
- **Chef's dinners ($46,200):** 35 seats at $150, 16 dinners a year, 55% sell-through. The nearest Seattle comparable is $130 all-inclusive with pairings; the low sell-through absorbs the pricing risk.
- **Breakfast and coffee ($30,240):** 20 sales a morning at $9, 4 mornings a week, 42 weeks. Sized to inn guests and a modest walk-up trade, alongside the coffee stand already operating next door.
- **Commissary ($3,200):** $32 an hour, 2 hours a week. Mid-range of Seattle-area commissary rates ($15–35 an hour), on a token schedule.
- **Misc kitchen and events ($6,820):** $210 a month of small hires plus $4,300 a year of private events.
